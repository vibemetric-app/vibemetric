# AI Native Score rubric

Behavioral guide and scoring policy updated: 2026-10-23. AI judges High, Medium, Low, or Unknown from inspected behavior, using the level that recurs most across the inspected tasks. The app calculates equal-weight dimension scores from those levels; AI does not assign numeric grades. Low means low-level practice where the competency applies; Unknown follows the shared Unknown handling rule.

## 1. Goal and scope

Assess how this person uses AI in real work. Return a diagnostic card that helps the person recognize their way of working with AI and choose useful changes to it. An unfamiliar reader, including a developer-tool maker, should understand the person's AI-use practices through concrete examples: how they direct and equip AI, judge its work, and intervene, and what those practices accomplish. Describe those practices using the observation guide below.

Investigate local AI conversation and execution records directly, without subagents, under the run's supplied reading target, reading deadline, and access limits. All accessible history is in scope, across projects, tools, and dates, including sessions since the previous assessment. Use indexes to locate relevant records, then inspect connected actions and outcomes. Prioritize unresolved dimensions while continuing toward the supplied reading target; there is no per-competency task quota or cap. Report actual examined and unexamined scope when records or time are insufficient.

Investigate Memory & Knowledge separately from model, token, and cost searches. Across available tools and date ranges, including older records, search message bodies for requests to remember, save, review, correct, or remove information. Inspect surrounding passages, actual storage or modification calls, and results; trace the content or path into later conversations to establish its application. Stored information can also start this investigation without an explicit request. Try other candidates when a chain is incomplete rather than stopping at a search hit count or one missing link. Injected memory qualifies when its content demonstrably guides a later answer or action; neither an explicit read call nor a full reread of all records is required.

## 2. Behavioral competencies

Review all competencies in exactly 10 dimensions. Every defined competency stays in its dimension's denominator. Do not return not_applicable or not_assessable in a new assessment.

### Unknown handling

Do not return Unknown merely because the first search found no relevant material. Follow connected records and other relevant cases, and incorporate relevant evidence encountered while investigating other dimensions.

Judge each competency from as many relevant tasks as the reading budget allows. Do not stop at the first task that shows the practice: look for further tasks where the competency applies, both in records already read and in additional sessions, whether the practice there was strong, partial, or missing. A level rests on the work that recurs, not on one or two examples.

When no further investigation time remains or additional searches yield no more material, assess using what has already been inspected. Do not assign Unknown merely because the available material is incomplete or further investigation could not be finished.

Use Unknown only when the inspected material, considered together, provides no basis at all to judge that competency. When an inspected task falls under a competency's "When to assess" line, judge it from what it shows; little or no practice there is Low, not Unknown.

### Task evidence, aggregate levels, and app-calculated scores

1. Judge each inspected connected task as High, Medium, Low, or Unknown. High and Medium follow the defining behavior below. Low means the practice reached only a low level: absent, minimal, ineffective, or wrong where the task called for it.
2. Aggregate distinct inspected tasks, counting one intended outcome once across continuations, retries, and delegated pieces. A task is inspected when its request, action, and result were read, in a session digest or in the original transcript; a task judged from a digest counts the same as one judged from the original, and the original is opened only when the digest does not show the action or result. Let H, M, and L be High, Medium, and Low task counts and K=H+M+L. If K=0, return Unknown. Otherwise return the level that occurs most often. If two or more levels tie for most frequent, return Medium. High also requires H/K >= 2/3; a most frequent High below two thirds returns Medium. Use exact fractions, not rounded display percentages. This is a product rule, not a scientifically validated ability scale. Explain all three counts and percentages with a representative real directory/task and defining behavior for each nonzero group. Include zero counts and percentages, and state in ordinary language which level recurred most and, for High, whether it reached two thirds.
3. The app computes each dimension from N defined competencies: High=3 thirds, Medium=2 thirds, Low=1 third, Unknown=0. Dimension score = round(10 * sum(thirds) / (3 * N)), rounding halves upward once after summing the entire dimension. Unknown stays in N. Do not round individual contributions. The total is the sum of the ten displayed integer dimension scores out of 100. No extra deductions or redistribution apply. Return levels and reasons; omit score from the app's structured response. For a standalone report, execute this arithmetic in code rather than judging numbers subjectively. For example, Medium/Medium/Medium/Unknown yields 5/10, and Medium/Medium/Medium yields 7/10.
4. Keep UI level names in English: High, Medium, Low, Unknown. Medium and Low belong under Problem; only Unknown belongs under Not rated. Explain Unknown as an evidence limit, never an insult or a proven personal failure. Use status scored for every new dimension, including all-Unknown dimensions, which calculate to zero. Explain the inspected scope separately from ability. A scoring-policy change does not establish a change in practice.

### Investigation metadata

In the app response, return scope.unknownInvestigations with exactly one entry per Unknown: dimension, competencyID, status (not_found, incomplete, or unavailable), actual searches, and remainingLeads. Describe the investigation honestly. not_found records exhausted accessible leads and an empty remainingLeads list; incomplete records unread relevant leads, and unavailable records an access obstacle. Investigation limits do not prevent returning or saving an otherwise valid score card.

Show project names and relevant project-relative file paths only. Do not show personal prefixes such as /Users/<name>/ or /home/<name>/ in summaries, explanations, scope notes, or copied reports. Keep the full working paths internal to investigation, and preserve exact public source URLs.

### Shared judgment rules

These rules apply to every competency. Each dimension below adds only its own "When to assess" conditions.

1. **Judge actual actions and results.** A saved file, tool definition, installation, keyword, quoted example, automatically injected memory, request for evidence, or AI completion claim alone does not establish use, success, or review. Follow the connection to the actual action or result.
2. **Connect the evidence.** Establish connections through the same call, task, file, artifact, check, or revision; nearby timestamps and unrelated successes do not establish a workflow. Follow an intended outcome through its request, investigation, execution, and review, and count it once across continuing sessions, retries, and delegated pieces. Consider supporting and contrary evidence actually read, state the examined scope, and never fabricate cases or imply that unread records were examined.
3. **Low is a low level.** Low means the practice reached only a low level where the competency's "When to assess" line applies: absent, minimal, ineffective, or wrong. It does not require a demonstrated failure, an uncorrected harm, or proof that nothing was attempted. Sample by situation, not by practice: include ordinary tasks where the competency's situation is present even when the practice is absent, judge little or no practice there as Low, and leave out only tasks that contain nothing of that kind.
4. **Keep established levels.** Credit partial practice at the level it establishes. An unconfirmed later effect or missing later reuse limits the higher level without erasing the lower one already established. Do not invent incidents, failures, conflicts, later runs, or comparisons to create evidence.
5. **Credit the person and AI accurately.** Credit work the person performs or delegates based on actual execution; personal code writing and doing AI's routine work are unnecessary. Required approvals and genuine human decisions are not penalties and do not prevent High. Attribute human decisions separately from AI or automatic-loop decisions; delegation alone does not establish that the person examined alternatives. Copying material or using an AI-written draft is neither a fault nor proof of skill; credit what the person does with it. Adopted third-party, built-in, and AI-written tools count when they support the same behavior.
6. **Automation only where the competency asks for it.** Automation is part of High only where a competency explicitly assesses it: knowledge storage and maintenance, context maintenance, workflow maintenance, automated checks, and automatic model selection. An installed mechanism without observed application is insufficient. Automatic execution earns no separate credit for the same action, explicit instructions satisfy competencies that do not require automation, and personal judgment need not be automated.
7. **No proxies or required complexity.** Prompt length, conversation counts, installations, model names, prestige or price, agent counts, tool counts, file counts, downloads, stars, and PR counts do not establish a competency. Do not require incidents, multiple agents, several tools, multiple files, or gratuitous procedural changes. Deliberately retaining or simplifying a verified, suitable method can satisfy the relevant decision.
8. **One behavior, one credit.** The same record supports different competencies only when it establishes their distinct behaviors. Do not award the same save, maintenance edit, review, or correction again under another name, or split one incident to reward the same action twice. Use the dimension boundaries below.
9. **Judge current practice.** Trace practices still in effect at the assessment time. When later records establish that a practice changed and the new practice remains in use, describe that change and its later application; recency alone does not establish a change. One successful case does not establish a recurring habit across all work.
10. **Read levels as behaviors.** Read each competency's evidence and level definitions for the opportunity the task presents. Do not turn every sentence in a level description into a separate scored checkbox. Apply project screening before interpreting project records.

### Project screening: small-scale, one-off projects

Screen projects before interpreting observations so that intentionally lightweight work is not mistaken for evidence of weak ability. For example, a game made for fun in one sitting need not demonstrate the practices expected of a maintained service. Use **small-scale, one-off project** as the classification; do not infer eligibility from labels such as "side project" or "toy project."

For each project used as evidence, inspect and record the following four indicators together. These are contextual screening guides, not independent pass/fail tests or validated measures of ability or quality. Do not use fixed file-count or duration cutoffs, or classify by counting how many signals match.

| Indicator | What to inspect and record | Limited-scope signal |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Deployment and actual use | Check available deployment results, releases, distribution records, and subsequent use. Distinguish a deployment configuration or completion claim from a successful deployment. Record confirmed deployment, confirmed local-only work, or unknown, and whether actual use is established. | Records establish only local or temporary preview execution, with no subsequent real use established. A deployed demo alone does not establish sustained use; a repeatedly used local tool counts as real use. |
| Project-specific implementation scope | Inspect the file tree and representative files implementing the project's own behavior: screens, domain rules, APIs, data processing, or integrations. Set aside unchanged scaffold files, generic framework setup, configuration, lockfiles, dependencies, generated code, build output, static assets, documentation, and tests. Include a scaffold file if it was changed to implement project-specific behavior. Note the approximate number of implementation files and judge the visible amount and breadth of actual implementation directly. Neither 10 nor roughly 50 files is a cutoff; file splitting or bundling alone does not establish scope. | After setting aside boilerplate, the actual implementation is visibly small and narrowly scoped, such as a simple standalone game or a single experimental flow with little surrounding functionality. Interpret this alongside use and history, not as an exclusion on its own. |
| Git activity continuity | Inspect whether work repeatedly continues across multiple weeks, regardless of weekdays, weekends, or a daily streak. Examine bursts, gaps, and returns to the project, and record the relevant absolute date ranges. Judge the pattern from commit timing, not a count of commits or dates with "substantive changes"; no per-commit substantive-change test is required. Set aside clearly automated-only activity and inherited upstream history. When activity is sparse, consult use, release, and task records to distinguish occasional tinkering from maintenance of an already completed, useful project. | A brief initial burst followed by long gaps and isolated returns can suggest occasional experimentation when the other evidence agrees. Sparse commits alone do not establish this: a working project that remains in use and receives only occasional maintenance is not a small-scale, one-off project for that reason. |
| Git history span | Record the first and last project work dates, elapsed calendar days, and the distribution of activity within that span. Use this project's own history, not inherited template or upstream history. Distinguish a short creation burst, repeated work over time, and a completed project followed by maintenance. | Work is confined to a short creation period with little evidence of continued development or use. A long first-to-last interval with isolated commits does not by itself establish sustained work, and a short history does not by itself establish an experiment. Apply no fixed duration cutoff. |

Classify a project as a **small-scale, one-off project** only when deployment and actual use, implementation scope, commit continuity, overall history, and the user's stated purpose jointly support that interpretation. Explain how the evidence combines; do not use a numerical cutoff or a "3 of 4" rule. Explicit requests such as "make a quick game for fun" can establish the limited scope of that task even inside a larger project; record the four indicators where applicable, but do not classify the entire repository from that task alone. Explicit commitments to a real deliverable, intended users, or ongoing use prevent automatic classification merely because the project is small or newly started. Account for completed projects that continue to serve their purpose with little additional development: repeated real use and occasional maintenance are evidence against treating them as casual experiments, even with long gaps between commits.

Missing Git history, a shallow or squashed history, unavailable deployment records, or an inaccessible source tree are **unknown**, not evidence that a signal is met. Consult available session or release records for corroboration. If scope remains unclear, report the unresolved classification and do not use presumed omissions in that work as negative evidence. A project that does not qualify as an experiment is not automatically proof of sustained or advanced practice.

After the minimum inspection needed for project screening, investigate classified small-scale, one-off projects **only for Planning & Specs, Tool Integration, and Efficiency & Cost**, judging those behaviors against the task's intended scope. For the other seven dimensions, exclude these projects from evidence discovery as well as interpretation: do not search their sessions, read further project records, or trace follow-up cases to find supporting or contrary examples. Scope searches to eligible projects wherever possible; discard incidental matches from excluded projects without expanding them. Apply this restriction to targeted searches, alternative-candidate searches, and corroboration alike. Evidence encountered during screening or the three permitted investigations must not be reused for the other seven dimensions. Exclude them from both supporting and contrary observation evidence for the other seven dimensions, including corroborating examples. Do not penalize the omission of practices unnecessary for the intended task, including within the three eligible dimensions. If no eligible evidence remains for another dimension, report that no eligible observations are available; exclusion does not establish a failed practice.

In the Scope note, briefly list screened projects with their deployment/use status, approximate implementation-file count and observed scope after excluding boilerplate, commit continuity pattern with absolute date ranges, absolute first/last work dates and history span, classification, and the combined reasoning, including maintenance context or unknowns. Preserve the distinction between a limited task and its larger project. Record the assessment reference date and time zone so that dates and the inspected history cutoff remain unambiguous.

Assess the intended outcome and working conditions established before the relevant implementation begins.

Read the initial request, instructions that were in effect, pre-execution investigation, and the adopted execution direction together. Credit an AI investigation or proposal when the person delegates that investigation or adopts its findings within the permitted scope. Do not require the person to write every detail, answer unnecessary questions, or approve each routine step. Successful implementation does not retroactively establish a clear plan.

#### When to assess

- **Essential project context.** Assess any task done inside a project that has its own conventions, business rules, or prior decisions an executor needs in order to work correctly, whether or not a project guide exists, was read, or covered them. Do not demand documentation of routine facts AI can reliably discover.
- **Context file organization.** Assess any task run inside a project where guidance that would help the executor exists or should exist, including tasks where no guide was loaded and the person supplied directions by hand. Evaluate only the tools and reading paths used in the work. Do not impose modular files on an already usable small guide or reward splitting content into imports that all load together.
- **Context maintenance.** Assess any task that changes commands, file locations, constraints, or decisions a project guide covers or should cover, or that runs into guidance that no longer matches the project, whether or not anyone updated the guide. AI can maintain confirmed facts automatically without inventing new policy.
- Judge content, delivery, and maintenance from their own evidence. Missing context, a failed reading route, contradictory content, and AI ignoring a rule it already read are different causes.

#### When to assess

- **Results and next steps.** Assess any task in which a command, test, build, review, or product observation produced output, whether or not that output changed what happened next. The person need not run the check or write the fix.
- **Execution recovery.** Assess any task in which something stopped execution (a failed command, missing dependency, permission denial, timeout, or limit), whether AI diagnosed it, handed the raw error to the person, or ignored it. An evidence-backed stop can be High even though the blocked operation stays unverified; this judges handling of the obstacle, not the blocked behavior.
- **Workflow maintenance.** Assess any task of a kind that recurs in the records (the same verification, check, or result-handling need in more than one session), whether a reusable loop handled it, the person re-requested it, or it was skipped. High requires observed later use without additional user intervention for each task.
- Retries need a diagnostic purpose or a supported transient-recovery reason and must respect applicable limits. Never reward endless retries, suppressed failed checks, widened permissions, unauthorized side effects, or claiming blocked work complete. Preserve successful evidence when resuming unless changed inputs require rechecking.

#### When to assess

- **AI proposal review.** Assess any task in which AI proposed or chose an approach, design, library, data change, or scope with consequences beyond the immediate edit, whether the person examined it, waved it through, or did not respond. Routine execution within an established decision needs no further comparison or approval, and multiple alternatives are unnecessary when the evidence already supports one suitable approach.
- **AI work correction.** Assess any task where AI's work drifted from what was asked, rested on an unverified explanation, or exceeded the asked scope, whether the person corrected it precisely, said only "try again", or accepted it as is. Correct autonomous work need not be interrupted. A redirection may begin with further investigation rather than a prescribed fix.
- **Assumption checks.** Assess any task in which investigation, output, or results contradicted something the person had assumed or instructed earlier, whether the person reconsidered, pushed on regardless, or did not notice. A reversal is not required; a supported decision to keep the original direction can demonstrate the same practice.
- Choosing among proposals, correcting AI's approach, and reconsidering one's own earlier position are separate decisions. One episode supports more than one competency only when it contains a distinct decision and evidence for each.

#### When to assess

- **Access control.** Assess any task in which AI ran with write, delete, network, credential, or production-affecting capability, or edited code that decides who may do what, whether the boundary was enforced, merely promised, or left open. - **Sensitive data protection.** Assess any task where secrets, credentials, personal or customer data, or confidential material were present in the files, environment, tool output, or conversation, or where the work builds a path that will carry such data, whether or not they were protected. Use synthetic markers for checks and never require disclosure of real secrets. An approved connector or secret store counts when the observed path preserves the boundary.
- **External input safety.** Assess any task in which AI read or acted on content someone else controls (fetched pages, documents, emails, issues, third-party skills or packages, or another model's output), whether that content was handled with separation or simply followed. A passing fixture establishes only that tested protection, not universal prompt-injection resistance. Existing parsers, tool policies, and framework escaping count when used and observed.
